Probability, Bayes' Rule & Naive Bayes

Classes 13 & 14  •  AIMA 13.2–13.5, 20.2

You will need this material a lot for the rest of the course — Bayesian networks, Markov models and HMMs all build directly on it. Go over it now rather than later.

Uncertainty

The general situation an agent finds itself in:

  • Observed variables (evidence): the agent knows certain things about the state of the world — sensor readings, symptoms.
  • Unobserved variables: the agent needs to reason about other aspects — where an object is, which disease is present.
  • Model: the agent knows something about how the known variables relate to the unknown ones.

Probabilistic reasoning gives us a framework for managing our beliefs and knowledge.

Random Variables

A random variable is some aspect of the world about which we (may) have uncertainty. We denote them with capital letters, and — just like variables in a CSP — they have domains.

Every distribution must satisfy:

\[ P(X = x) \geq 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \sum_{x} P(X = x) = 1 \]

Joint Distributions

A joint distribution over a set of random variables \( X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n \) specifies a real number for each assignment (each outcome):

\[ P(X_1 = x_1,\ X_2 = x_2,\ \dots,\ X_n = x_n) \qquad \text{written } P(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) \] \[ P(x_1, \dots, x_n) \geq 0 \qquad \sum_{(x_1 \dots x_n)} P(x_1, \dots, x_n) = 1 \]

Events and Marginalization

An event is a set \(E\) of outcomes. From a joint distribution we can calculate the probability of any event by summing the rows in it:

\[ P(E) = \sum_{(x_1 \dots x_n) \in E} P(x_1, \dots, x_n) \]

Typically the events we care about are partial assignments, like \( P(T = hot) \).

Quiz: Events

Given the joint distribution \( P(X,Y) \): (+x,+y) = 0.2, (+x,−y) = 0.3, (−x,+y) = 0.4, (−x,−y) = 0.1.

\[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \]

Adding 0.4 and 0.5 alone would double-count the (+x, −y) row.

Marginal Distributions

Marginal distributions are sub-tables which eliminate variables. Marginalization (summing out) combines collapsed rows by adding:

\[ P(X_1 = x_1) = \sum_{x_2} P(X_1 = x_1,\ X_2 = x_2) \]

From the T/W joint above: \( P(hot) = 0.4 + 0.1 = 0.5 \), \( P(sun) = 0.4 + 0.2 = 0.6 \), \( P(rain) = 0.1 + 0.3 = 0.4 \).

Quiz: Marginal Distributions

From the same \(P(X,Y)\): \( P(+x) = 0.5,\ P(-x) = 0.5 \) and \( P(+y) = 0.6,\ P(-y) = 0.4 \).

Conditional Probability

A simple relation between joint and conditional probabilities — in fact this is taken as the definition of a conditional probability:

\[ P(a \mid b) = \frac{P(a, b)}{P(b)} \]

Conditional distributions are probability distributions over some variables given fixed values of others. From the T/W joint:

\[ P(W = sun \mid T = cold) = \frac{P(sun, cold)}{P(cold)} = \frac{0.2}{0.5} = 0.4 \] \[ P(W = rain \mid T = cold) = \frac{0.3}{0.5} = 0.6 \]

Note the conditional distribution is itself a proper distribution — 0.4 + 0.6 = 1.

Probabilistic Inference

Probabilistic inference means computing a desired probability from other known probabilities (e.g. a conditional from a joint). We generally compute conditional probabilities — these represent the agent's beliefs given the evidence — and beliefs change as evidence arrives:

  • \[ P(\text{on time} \mid \text{no reported accidents}) = 0.90 \]
  • \[ P(\text{on time} \mid \text{no accidents, 5 a.m.}) = 0.95 \]
  • \[ P(\text{on time} \mid \text{no accidents, 5 a.m., raining}) = 0.80 \]

Product Rule and Chain Rule

The Product Rule

Sometimes we have conditional distributions but want the joint. Rearranging the definition of conditional probability:

\[ P(a, b) = P(a \mid b)\, P(b) \]

Example. Given \( P(W) \) and \( P(D \mid W) \), multiply to get the joint \( P(D, W) \):

DW\(P(D \mid W)\)\(P(W)\)\(P(D, W)\)
wetsun0.10.80.08
drysun0.90.80.72
wetrain0.70.20.14
dryrain0.30.20.06

The Chain Rule

More generally, we can always write any joint distribution as an incremental product of conditional distributions:

\[ P(x_1, x_2, x_3) = P(x_1)\, P(x_2 \mid x_1)\, P(x_3 \mid x_1, x_2) \] \[ P(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = \prod_i P(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1}) \]

This is always true — no independence assumption needed. It is the foundation that Bayes' nets and Naive Bayes both build on.

Bayes' Rule

There are two ways to factor a joint distribution over two variables:

\[ P(a, b) = P(a \mid b)\, P(b) = P(b \mid a)\, P(a) \]

Dividing, we get Bayes' Rule:

\[ P(a \mid b) = \frac{P(b \mid a)\, P(a)}{P(b)} \]

Why is this helpful? It lets us build one conditional from its reverse. Often one conditional is tricky but the other one is simple — and it is the foundation of many systems (automatic speech recognition, machine translation). It is in the running for the most important equation in AI.

Inference with Bayes' Rule

The classic use is getting a diagnostic probability from a causal one:

\[ P(\text{cause} \mid \text{effect}) = \frac{P(\text{effect} \mid \text{cause})\, P(\text{cause})}{P(\text{effect})} \]

Example. \(M\): meningitis, \(S\): stiff neck. Given \( P(+m) = 0.0001 \), \( P(+s \mid +m) = 0.8 \), \( P(+s \mid -m) = 0.01 \):

\[ P(+m \mid +s) = \frac{P(+s \mid +m) P(+m)}{P(+s \mid +m)P(+m) + P(+s \mid -m)P(-m)} = \frac{0.8 \times 0.0001}{0.8 \times 0.0001 + 0.01 \times 0.9999} \approx 0.0079 \]
  • Note: the posterior probability of meningitis is still very small — under 1% — because the prior is tiny. A strong likelihood cannot rescue a rare cause on its own.
  • Note: you should still get stiff necks checked out! Why? Because the cost of missing meningitis is enormous. Probability alone does not make the decision — utility does.

Quiz: Bayes' Rule

Given \( P(W) \) = {sun 0.8, rain 0.2} and \( P(D \mid W) \) as in the product-rule table, what is \( P(W \mid dry) \)?

\[ P(dry) = 0.72 + 0.06 = 0.78 \] \[ P(sun \mid dry) = \frac{0.72}{0.78} = \frac{12}{13} \approx 0.923 \qquad P(rain \mid dry) = \frac{0.06}{0.78} = \frac{1}{13} \approx 0.077 \]

Independence

Two variables are independent if their joint distribution factors into a product of two simpler distributions:

\[ \forall x, y:\ P(x, y) = P(x)\, P(y) \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad \forall x, y:\ P(x \mid y) = P(x) \] \[ \text{written } X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \]

Independence is a simplifying modelling assumption. Empirical joint distributions are at best "close" to independent.

Example: Independence?

With marginals \(P(T)\) = {hot 0.5, cold 0.5} and \(P(W)\) = {sun 0.6, rain 0.4}:

TWJoint 1Joint 2\(P(T)P(W)\)

coldsun0.20.30.30
coldrain0.30.20.20

Joint 1 is not independent — 0.4 ≠ 0.5 × 0.6. Joint 2 is independent — every cell equals the product of its marginals.

N fair, independent coin flips: the full joint needs \(2^N\) numbers, but independence means you only need \(N\) numbers (one per flip). That collapse is exactly what we are hunting for.

Conditional Independence

Unconditional (absolute) independence is very rare. Conditional independence is our most basic and robust form of knowledge about uncertain environments.

Consider \( P(\text{Toothache}, \text{Cavity}, \text{Catch}) \). If I have a cavity, the probability that the probe catches in it doesn't depend on whether I have a toothache:

  • \[ P(+catch \mid +toothache, +cavity) = P(+catch \mid +cavity) \]
  • The same holds if I don't have a cavity: \[ P(+catch \mid +toothache, -cavity) = P(+catch \mid -cavity) \]
  • So Catch is conditionally independent of Toothache given Cavity.

Formally, \(X\) is conditionally independent of \(Y\) given \(Z\) if and only if:

\[ \forall x,y,z:\ P(x, y \mid z) = P(x \mid z)\, P(y \mid z) \] \[ \text{equivalently} \qquad \forall x,y,z:\ P(x \mid z, y) = P(x \mid z) \]

These statements are equivalent and each can be derived from the other easily.

Think about these domains: Traffic, Umbrella, Raining — is Traffic independent of Umbrella given Raining? And Fire, Smoke, Alarm — is Alarm independent of Fire given Smoke? In each case the middle variable "explains away" the connection between the other two.

Conditional Independence and the Chain Rule

Start from the chain rule, which is always valid:

\[ P(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n) = \prod_i P(X_i \mid X_1 \dots X_{i-1}) \]

Now assume conditional independences and the conditioning sets shrink dramatically. Bayes' nets / graphical models are the machinery for expressing exactly which conditional independence assumptions we are making.

Bayes' Nets: The Big Picture

Graphical Model Notation

  • Nodes: variables (with domains). Can be assigned (observed) or unassigned (unobserved).
  • Arcs: interactions. Similar to CSP constraints; they indicate "direct influence" between variables, and formally they encode conditional independence.
  • For now, imagine that arrows mean direct causation — though in general they don't.

Bayes' Net Semantics

A Bayes net is topology (graph) + local conditional probabilities:

  • A set of nodes, one per variable \(X\).
  • A directed, acyclic graph.
  • A conditional distribution for each node — a collection of distributions over \(X\), one for each combination of the parents' values. This is the CPT (conditional probability table), a description of a noisy "causal" process.

Probabilities in Bayes' Nets

Bayes' nets implicitly encode joint distributions as a product of local conditional distributions. To see what probability a BN gives to a full assignment, multiply all the relevant conditionals together:

\[ P(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(x_i \mid \text{parents}(X_i)) \]

Why is this guaranteed to give a proper joint distribution? Because it is the chain rule (always valid) plus the assumed conditional independences. Not every BN can represent every joint distribution — the topology enforces certain conditional independencies.

Example: Traffic. \(R\) = it rains, \(T\) = there is traffic, with \(R \rightarrow T\).

Model-Based Classification

  • Build a model (e.g. a Bayes' net) where both the label and the features are random variables.
  • Instantiate any observed features.
  • Query for the distribution of the label conditioned on the features.

The challenges: what structure should the BN have, and how should we learn its parameters?

The Naive Bayes Model

Naive Bayes answers the structure question in the simplest possible way: one parent (the label \(Y\)) pointing at every feature, and no arcs among the features.

\[ P(Y, F_1, F_2, \dots, F_n) = P(Y) \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(F_i \mid Y) \]

We only have to specify how each feature depends on the class. The "naive" part is the assumption that the features are conditionally independent given the label.

Why this matters: parameter counts

ModelNumber of parametersGrowth
Full joint \(P(Y, F_1 \dots F_n)\)\(|Y| \times |F|^n\) valuesexponential in \(n\)
Naive Bayes\(|Y|\) for the prior + \(n \times |F| \times |Y|\) for the CPTslinear in \(n\)

The model is very simplistic, but often works anyway. That combination — wrong assumptions, useful answers — is why Naive Bayes is still a real tool and not just a teaching example.

Inference for Naive Bayes

Goal: compute the posterior distribution over the label variable \(Y\) given evidence \(f_1 \dots f_n\).

  1. Step 1: get the joint probability of the label and the evidence, for each label: \[ P(y, f_1 \dots f_n) = P(y) \prod_i P(f_i \mid y) \]
  2. Step 2: sum over labels to get the probability of the evidence: \[ P(f_1 \dots f_n) = \sum_y P(y, f_1 \dots f_n) \]
  3. Step 3: normalize — divide Step 1 by Step 2: \[ P(y \mid f_1 \dots f_n) = \frac{P(y, f_1 \dots f_n)}{P(f_1 \dots f_n)} \]

In practice \(P(f_1 \dots f_n)\) is the same for every label, so it is common to compute the unnormalized scores and normalize only at the end. That constant is often written \(\alpha\).

What do we need in order to use Naive Bayes?

  • An inference method — the three steps above. Nothing new here.
  • Estimates of the local conditional probability tables: \(P(Y)\), the prior over labels, and \(P(F_i \mid Y)\) for each feature. These are collectively the parameters of the model, denoted \(\theta\).

Where do the parameters come from? Training data counts — which is what the worked examples below do.

Building up: a single-evidence classifier

Before the full model, take just one feature. Using the 14-day weather dataset below with only Humidity as evidence:

  • Priors: \(P(play = yes) = 9/14\), \(P(play = no) = 5/14\).
  • How many times is Humidity = normal out of all 9 Yes's? 6. So \(P(normal \mid yes) = 6/9\).
  • How many times is Humidity = normal out of all 5 No's? 1. So \(P(normal \mid no) = 1/5\).
  • Similarly \(P(high \mid yes) = 3/9\) and \(P(high \mid no) = 4/5\).

Today is a high humidity day — what is the probability we play?

\[ P(yes \mid high) = P(yes)P(high \mid yes)\,\alpha = \tfrac{9}{14} \cdot \tfrac{3}{9} \cdot \alpha = \tfrac{3}{14}\alpha \] \[ P(no \mid high) = P(no)P(high \mid no)\,\alpha = \tfrac{5}{14} \cdot \tfrac{4}{5} \cdot \alpha = \tfrac{4}{14}\alpha \]

So the odds are 4:3 against playing given high humidity — versus 5:9 against before any evidence. Observing high humidity moved the belief substantially, and we never needed \(P(high)\) itself.

Query 1 — Sunny, Hot, Normal humidity, no wind

\[ P(Yes \mid today) = \frac{P(Sunny \mid Yes)P(Hot \mid Yes)P(Normal \mid Yes)P(NoWind \mid Yes)P(Yes)}{P(today)} \]

\(P(today)\) is common to both classes, so we ignore it and compute proportional scores:

\[ P(Yes \mid today) \propto \tfrac{3}{9} \cdot \tfrac{2}{9} \cdot \tfrac{6}{9} \cdot \tfrac{6}{9} \cdot \tfrac{9}{14} \approx 0.0212 \] \[ P(No \mid today) \propto \tfrac{2}{5} \cdot \tfrac{2}{5} \cdot \tfrac{1}{5} \cdot \tfrac{2}{5} \cdot \tfrac{5}{14} \approx 0.0046 \]

The two posteriors must sum to 1, so normalize:

\[ P(Yes \mid today) = \frac{0.0212}{0.0212 + 0.0046} \approx \mathbf{0.8217} \qquad P(No \mid today) = \frac{0.0046}{0.0212 + 0.0046} \approx \mathbf{0.1783} \]

Smoothing

The problem: unseen events

Look again at \(P(Overcast \mid No) = 0/5\). If a test day is Overcast, then every term in the No product is multiplied by zero:

\[ P(No \mid \text{Overcast}, \dots) \propto \tfrac{2}{5} \cdot \dots \cdot \mathbf{0} \cdot \dots = 0 \]

The classifier becomes absolutely certain the answer is Yes, on the strength of five training rows that merely happened not to include an overcast non-golf day. This is overfitting: one unobserved combination vetoes an entire class no matter what the other features say.

Laplace smoothing

Laplace's estimate: pretend you saw every outcome one more time than you actually did. Extended: pretend you saw every outcome \(k\) extra times.

\[ P_{LAP,k}(x) = \frac{c(x) + k}{N + k|X|} \]

and for conditionals — smooth each condition independently:

\[ P_{LAP,k}(x \mid y) = \frac{c(x, y) + k}{c(y) + k|X|} \]
  • \(c(x)\) is the count of \(x\), \(N\) is the total count, and \(|X|\) is the number of values the variable can take — not the number of training rows.
  • \(k\) is the strength of the prior. Laplace with \(k = 0\) is just the unsmoothed count ratio.

Example. You draw three balls: red, red, blue. So \(N = 3\), \(|X| = 2\):

\(k\)\(P(red)\)\(P(blue)\)Effect
02/3 ≈ 0.6671/3 ≈ 0.333raw counts
13/5 = 0.6002/5 = 0.400pulled toward uniform
100102/203 ≈ 0.502101/203 ≈ 0.498almost entirely uniform

As \(k\) grows the data matters less and the uniform prior matters more.

\(P(Speed{=}High \mid \cdot)\)3 of 5 Yes, 2 of 5 No\(\frac{3+1}{5+2} = \frac{4}{7}\)\(\frac{2+1}{5+2} = \frac{3}{7}\)
\(P(Stamina{=}High \mid \cdot)\)4 of 5 Yes, 1 of 5 No\(\frac{4+1}{5+2} = \frac{5}{7}\)\(\frac{1+1}{5+2} = \frac{2}{7}\)

Note the smoothing earning its keep: no selected student is Short, so the raw \(P(Short \mid yes)\) is 0 and would have forced the Yes score to zero outright.

\[ P(yes, 14, short, high, high) = \tfrac{6}{12} \cdot \tfrac{3}{8} \cdot \tfrac{1}{8} \cdot \tfrac{4}{7} \cdot \tfrac{5}{7} = \frac{15}{32 \cdot 49} \approx 0.0096 \] \[ P(no, 14, short, high, high) = \tfrac{6}{12} \cdot \tfrac{3}{8} \cdot \tfrac{4}{8} \cdot \tfrac{3}{7} \cdot \tfrac{2}{7} = \frac{9}{16 \cdot 49} \approx 0.0115 \]

Normalizing: \(P(yes \mid x) = \frac{15}{33} = \frac{5}{11} \approx 45.5\%\) and \(P(no \mid x) = \frac{18}{33} = \frac{6}{11} \approx 54.5\%\).

Answer: the student is NOT likely to be selected, since \(0.0115 > 0.0096\). It is a close call, and being Short is what decides it.

Exam Checklist

  • Read a probability off a joint table; compute an event by summing rows; remember inclusion–exclusion for OR.
  • Marginalize by summing out a variable; condition by dividing by the evidence probability.
  • State and apply the product rule, chain rule and Bayes' rule; get a diagnostic probability from a causal one.
  • Test independence by checking \(P(x,y) = P(x)P(y)\) for every cell; state conditional independence both ways.
  • Write the Naive Bayes factorization and say why the parameter count is linear rather than exponential in \(n\).
  • Build likelihood tables from a dataset by counting, then classify a query and normalize at the end.
  • Apply Laplace smoothing — and be careful that \(|X|\) is the number of values of that feature, and that the denominator for a conditional is \(c(y) + k|X|\), not \(N + k|X|\).
